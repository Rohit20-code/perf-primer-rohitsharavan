## Self-check answers

**P3.1: After a load test that adds items to baskets, how would you prove in SQL how many baskets it created?**
`SELECT COUNT(*)` on the baskets table, filtered to only what the test made: the test's time window, or better, the test users' IDs. Or count before and after and subtract. Then compare with the number of successful "create basket" requests JMeter reports. If they don't match, something got lost.

**P3.2: What does `df.groupby('label')['elapsed'].quantile(0.95)` return?**
The 95th-percentile response time **for each request type**. JMeter results have a `label` column (the sampler name, e.g. "Login", "Add to basket") and `elapsed` (response time in ms). This groups the rows by label, takes the elapsed column, and gives back one p95 value per label, as a Series indexed by label.

**P3.3: Why do performance reports use p95 rather than the average?**
The average mixes the fast majority with the slow few into a number nobody experienced, and outliers distort it. p95 says "95% of requests were at least this fast". In my simulation, when slow requests went from 1% to 6%, p95 jumped from 368 to 1685 ms while the median didn't move.

## Learned
- `GROUP BY` collapses rows; an inner `JOIN` silently drops rows with no match, while `LEFT JOIN … IS NULL` finds them.
- Typing `python` ran the wrong Python here (an MSYS2 one without pip). `where python` showed why: it's first on PATH. Using `py` picked the right one, the PATH lesson from P1.2 happening for real.
- Average, median and percentiles answer different questions, and the high percentiles are the ones that notice a growing slow tail.
